# Punctuation: marks, set-off phrases, address and parenthetical words

Source: Kvachadze, Modern Georgian Syntax (1988).
Kvachadze is from 1988, so its rules reflect the literary norm of that time.

## Series and like members

- Separate like members with commas, but write no comma before the `და` or `თუ` that joins them. Other
  conjunctions do not cancel the comma: keep it before `მაგრამ` and `ხოლო`. Parallel subordinate clauses are also
  separated by commas unless joined; here the book names only `და`.
  Example: ხე, ქვა და ტყე · ხე, ბუჩქი თუ ბალახი · ❌ ხე, ქვა, და ტყე · პატარა, მაგრამ ლამაზი სახლი.
  (Kvachadze §203, a 1 and a 3)
- The semicolon always separates like elements, like members or like clauses, and stands midway between the full
  stop and the comma. Use it in three main cases: clauses that are somewhat independent and linked less tightly
  than a comma would show, or very extended clauses, especially with commas inside them; many extended parallel
  subordinate clauses, the more so with commas inside; a long sentence with like members, to separate one whole
  block of like members from the next. Example: ზღვა ღელავდა; ქარი ძლიერდებოდა. (Kvachadze §204)

## Set-off elements: the comma pair

- The comma also sets off various elements, whether a word, a word group or a clause. It is used as a pair, one
  before and one after the element, and the two are always used together. At the start or end of the sentence a
  single comma is written, and it both divides and sets off the element.
  Example: ჩემი ძმა, ექიმი, მოვიდა · რასაკვირველია, მოვა. (Kvachadze §203, b)
- The comma sets off a detached modifier, a detached circumstance (adverbial) and an apposition. The apposition
  does not always take commas: where a dash sets it off, it is not read as one more like member.
  Example: ბიჭი, სახლში დაბრუნებული, დაიძინა · ხვალ, კვირას, წავალთ ·
  თბილისი, საქართველოს დედაქალაქი, იზრდება. (Kvachadze §203, b 1-3 and §206, 5)
- The comma also sets off an address, an inserted word, phrase or clause, an interjection, and a response word
  such as `არა` or `დიაღ`, which is cut off from the sentence after it or, rarely, from the sentence before it.
  For the address and the interjection, the cases that need an exclamation mark are excepted.
  Example: გიორგი, მოდი აქ · ოჰ, ვერ მოვასწარი · ის, რასაკვირველია, მოვა · ეს, როგორც ვთქვით, ახალია ·
  არა, ვერ მოვალ. (Kvachadze §203, b 4-7)

## Attributes that are set off

- An attribute that follows its noun and has its own dependent words (a participle or an adjective) is usually set
  off. A single bare attribute after the noun can stay unmarked (it gains a comma once a dependent word is
  added), but it can also be set off, the more so when the noun already has an attribute in front of it. Two or
  more attributes after the noun, bare or extended, are usually set off, and this is required when another
  attribute stands before the noun. When none stands before it, several bare following attributes may stay without
  commas. In writing, the book marks a set-off attribute with a comma, a colon or a dash, and rarely with
  parentheses. Example: ფოთოლი, ქარისგან დაგლეჯილი, დაეცა · დიდი სახლი ჩანდა, ნათელი.
  (Kvachadze §149, §150)
- As a rule a pronoun does not take an ordinary attribute in a word pair, so one or more modifiers tied to a
  pronoun subject or object (`ის`, `მე`) are set off, before or after the pronoun. An attribute that also carries a
  circumstance (time, cause, concession, condition) is always set off.
  Example: ის, დაღლილი, დაბრუნდა · გაცივებული, ლევანი სახლში დარჩა. (Kvachadze §149, §150)
- After a nominalised `უფროსი` / `უმცროსი`, the name that follows is set off, because these words cannot attach to
  a personal name as its attribute. If a common noun stands in place of the name, there is no set-off, since the
  adjective is then an ordinary attribute of that noun.
  Example: უფროსმა, ლევანმა, სწავლა დაამთავრა · უფროსმა ძმამ სწავლა დაამთავრა. (Kvachadze §149)

## Apposition

- A set-off apposition adds explaining or clarifying information about a sentence member (a subject, an object,
  the nominal part of a predicate, an address). It is usually a noun or a word used as a noun, rarely a pronoun;
  its head can be a noun, a nominalised word or a pronoun. It matches its head in case and number, and if the head
  has a postposition, the apposition as a rule repeats it. It usually follows the head, rarely stands before it,
  and is rarely separated from it. It may be bare or extended by dependent words. In writing it is marked with a
  comma or commas, or a dash or dashes, and sometimes it is put in parentheses.
  Example: ექიმს, გიორგის ძმას, დაურეკეს · ჩვენთვის, მასწავლებლებისთვის, ეს რთულია. (Kvachadze §151)
- A noun that defines a second noun, usually a proper name, is a plain apposition and is written without commas
  in the book's examples (the page states no comma rule in words). When a profession noun of this kind is moved
  after the name, it becomes set off. Example: პროფესორი ბერიძე · ჟურნალი „ცისკარი“. (Kvachadze §149, §151)
- A set-off apposition can stand without a link or be joined to its head by a linking word or particle, among
  them `ე. ი.`, `ანუ`, `როგორც`, `სახელდობრ`, `მაგალითად`, `სახელად`, `გვარად`, `განსაკუთრებით`. In the book's
  examples the linking word sits inside the punctuation together with the apposition, and no comma follows the
  linking word itself (shown by the examples, not stated as a rule).
  Example: კაცი, სახელად ლევანი, მოვიდა · ქალები, განსაკუთრებით ახალგაზრდები, მოვიდნენ. (Kvachadze §151)

## Set-off adverbials and a clarifying predicate

- Setting an adverbial off from the predicate in the strict sense is rare and is done for style, for more
  expressive force. The widespread type is a second adverbial that explains or narrows an earlier one. Set-off
  place, time and manner adverbials are usual; set-off cause and purpose adverbials are very rare. A place
  adverbial taken alone is seldom set off; it is often set off when a second place phrase narrows or explains the
  first, and sometimes several follow in a row. Time adverbials are set off less often than place ones: a single
  time phrase can be set off, but it is comparatively more common for a time phrase to narrow or explain an
  earlier one. In fiction, manner adverbials are set off fairly widely, one or several, or one that narrows or
  completes an earlier manner phrase.
  Example: ქვევით, კედელთან, იდგა მაგიდა · ზაფხულში, განსაკუთრებით აგვისტოში, აქ ცხელა ·
  ის მუშაობდა, ჩუმად, თავდაუზოგავად. (Kvachadze §152)
- The predicate is a main member, so in the strict sense it cannot be set off; "set off" applies loosely to a
  further predicate that explains or narrows it. It can go with a simple verb or with the nominal part of a
  compound predicate, sometimes several go with one predicate, and it may be extended by its own dependent words
  or have its own subject. It can stand without a link or be joined with `ანუ` or `ე. ი.` Most of the book's
  examples mark it with a comma; a dash, parentheses and a colon before a series also appear. The added predicate
  comes after the first one. Example: ამინდი ცუდი იყო, აუტანელი · ბოლოს წავიდა — გაიქცა. (Kvachadze §153)
- `გარდა` usually takes the genitive and has two senses, leaving something out ("except") and adding ("besides").
  `მეტი` is used in the sense of `გარდა` and also takes the genitive; its examples mean "except".
  `მიუხედავად` takes the genitive too, and the whole phrase carries a concession. The book treats these phrases
  as set-off words with the sense of an object or an adverbial; school grammars usually call them parenthetical.
  A dependent clause sometimes follows. The book's examples set `გარდა` and `მიუხედავად` phrases off with
  commas, while one of the two `მეტი` examples has no comma around the phrase. The genitive appears both after
  `გარდა` (long ending) and before it (short ending, `ერთის გარდა`).
  Example: გარდა ამისა, ყველა დათანხმდა · ყველა, გარდა ლევანისა, წავიდა · ერთის გარდა, ყველა წავიდა ·
  ერთის მეტი. (Kvachadze §154)

## Address and its form

- An address is not a sentence member and, with any words attached to it, stands fully apart from the sentence.
  It has no fixed place: it can open a sentence, sit inside it, close it, or stand alone.
  - It usually comes first when it is meant to draw attention (appeals, speeches, private letters) or when it is
    repeated.
  - It usually comes last in greeting and farewell formulas, in questions with a question word and in
    exclamations of praise, and as a rule it follows a reply word such as `არა` or `დიახ`.
  - It usually comes inside when the subject or object is a second-person pronoun, between repeated members or
    clauses, between a main and a dependent clause, and when it shows warmth in a longer sentence.
  - A one-member sentence, or one whose parts cannot be split, takes it only first or last.
  (Kvachadze §155)
- An address standing alone is said with a raised tone as a call, and the book's examples mostly write it with
  `!`. At the start of a sentence it has a higher tone than the sentence: `!` after it means a long pause and
  high tone, a comma a short pause and lower tone. Inside and at the end it is shown between commas or after a
  comma (shown by examples, not stated as a rule).
  Example: მეგობარო! მოდი აქ · მეგობარო, მოდი აქ · მოდი, მეგობარო, დაჯექი · მოდი აქ, მეგობარო!
  (Kvachadze §155)
- The address has its own form, the vocative case. A noun in it either gets `ო` or `ვ` added, or appears as the
  bare stem and then looks the same as the nominative. The one example of the bare type is the vowel-final `დედა`;
  the book does not say which nouns take which form. An agreeing attribute (a possessive or an adjective) also
  stands in the vocative. A governed attribute keeps its genitive (`ბუნების ძალნო`), and the book also shows a
  mixed type, a genitive placed after the noun that adds the vocative ending (`ძალნო ბუნებისანო`).
  Example: მზეო · წვიმავ · დედა · ბნელო ღამევ · ჩემო ჭაბუკო მკითხველო. (Kvachadze §155)

## Parenthetical words

- Affectionate expressions and some oath formulas are parenthetical (`გენაცვალე`, `გეთაყვა`, `შენი ჭირიმე`,
  `თქვენმა მზემ`, `ღმერთმანი`, `ძმობამ` and others) and have the force of an interjection. The book's examples
  set them off with commas at the start, inside or at the end of a sentence (shown by examples, not stated as a
  rule). Example: მოდი, გენაცვალე, აქ · ღმერთმანი, არ ვიცოდი · დამეხმარე, გეთაყვა. (Kvachadze §156)

## Colon

- Put a colon before a series of like members when a generalizing word stands at the head of the series. If a word
  pointing to an enumeration follows the generalizing word (`სახელდობრ`, `მაგალითად`, `როგორიცაა`), a comma goes
  before that word and the colon after it. The colon can also precede a series without a generalizing word, when
  the writer wants to signal an enumeration.
  Example: სამი ფერი: წითელი, თეთრი, შავი · ხილი, მაგალითად: ვაშლი და მსხალი. (Kvachadze §205, 1 and remarks 1-2)
- In a complex sentence whose parts are joined without a connector, put a colon after the first part when the
  second part states the cause, purpose or result of the first, or gives a fuller restatement of the first part's
  meaning or of one of its members. Example: ხვალ ვერ მოვალ: ავად ვარ. (Kvachadze §205, 2)

## Dash

- A dash is written where a predicate has been omitted, and where the verbal part of a compound predicate (the
  linking verb) has been omitted. All the book's examples are parallel statements, with the dash in a later part
  and the missing word understood from an earlier part; it gives no example of a bare "X — Y" sentence with no
  such earlier part. Example: ნიკამ ყავა დალია, ანამ — ჩაი · ქალაქი დიდია, სოფელი — პატარა. (Kvachadze §206, 1-2)
- When a generalizing word follows a series of like members, put a dash before that word. (For a generalizing
  word placed before the series, the colon rule applies.) Example: ხე, ქვა, ტყე — ყველაფერი გაიყინა.
  (Kvachadze §206, 3)
- Each participant's utterance in a dialogue usually begins with a dash (and a new paragraph). A dash also sets the
  author's words off from direct speech. All the book's three examples have the speech ending in `!`, then a
  dash, then the author's words; it shows no speech ending in a question mark or a plain statement, so it does not
  say what mark goes there. Example: — სად მიდიხარ? · — სახლში მივდივარ. · — სახლისკენ! — დაიძახა მან.
  (Kvachadze §206, 10-11)

## Ellipsis, parentheses, quotation marks

- The ellipsis marks an unfinished or cut-off utterance, a sentence or word spoken in broken pieces, an unexpected
  jump from one subject to another, and similar cases, arising from the author's agitation, heavy state of mind or
  surge of feeling, from others cutting in, and the like. It is usually three dots in a row and can be written
  together with `?` or `!`; the book's examples print `!..` and `?!.`. It also marks a stammer, and a word or words
  left out of a quotation, an omission that reading aloud does not show by intonation.
  Example: მას... მას... · მოვდივარ!.. · ს...საოცარია! (as printed in the book). (Kvachadze §202)
- Parentheses enclose a word or word group that explains the preceding word, and an inserted sentence (especially
  an extended one) in which the author adds information, an explanation or a remark in passing. Sometimes they
  enclose a part of the sentence itself, such as a subordinate clause, or a word or word group tied to the
  sentence grammatically. They also hold the source of a passage cited in the text.
  Example: ფაცხა (პატარა ქოხი) დაინგრა. (Kvachadze §207, 1-3 and remark)
- Quotation marks enclose another person's words quoted unchanged, word for word; sometimes the author says whose
  words they are, and sometimes the marks alone show it. The author's own earlier words quoted word for word also
  go in them. They also enclose a word or words used ironically or in a sense other than the usual one, and the
  titles of books, journals, newspapers, other works and the like. Use the Georgian low-high marks.
  Example: რომანი „მწვანე ბაღი“ · ის „დიდი მეცნიერი“ ისევ შეცდა. (Kvachadze §208, 1-2 and remark)
